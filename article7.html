Descartes' Meditations

By: Ahmed Mustafa

Descartes’ Meditations on First Philosophy is considered one of the most groundbreaking philosophical works of all time, containing one of the few philosophical quotes you will ever come to see wall-painted in streets: “I am thinking, therefore I am.” In this set of six meditations, Descartes lit the light on what was hidden behind the prejudices of our experiences and the tradition of early philosophers to yield—for many historians and philosophers—a turning point in philosophy. Moreover, he challenges the very items of knowledge, as they are to him ill-grounded and open to doubt, impelling him to endow us with a lasting epistemological enterprise in order to know anything with a great deal of confidence. To Descartes, the radical endeavor for a satisfactory grounding of philosophy is necessary for an all-embracing theory of the world, expanding beyond the scope of philosophy:

“I realized that it was necessary, once in the course of my life, to demolish everything completely and start again right from the foundations if I wanted to establish anything at all in the sciences that was stable and likely to last.”

Philosophers could be distinguished into two classes: Particularists and Methodists. Particularists are willing to trust our perceived intuitions about the world, while Methodists are less agreeable to associate any certainty upon the senses. Descartes is certainly a Methodist in this regard, for his first meditation introduces what he calls “The Method of Doubt”. Descartes defines perfect knowledge upon doubt, for to have certainty about anything is to be undoubtable. Although the notion of doubt and certainty seems to be psychological, it is grounded by what is utterly clear to the mind’s eye by pain of contradiction. In the course of the book, he associates certainty with clarity and distinctiveness. Although no direct definitions are present in the book, in his later works—and through a well-grounded interpretation of his work—a statement about something is called clear if it’s accessible to the attentive mind, and distinct if it’s unique and not mixed with any perception. Now the reader must bear in mind that we have not required—yet—that a clear and distinct idea of something must be true; i.e., to be the case.

Dreams are merely a noetic experience. Nonetheless, a quick reminder of how real dreams feel, how intricate people and objects appear, and how sensation inputs resemble (presumably) our real world. Indeed, there is no subtle reason that would make me think I am not dreaming at this very moment. And all the images derived from this world might not be real, and the concepts derived from experience might simply be falsehoods. In order to reach any form of certainty (i.e., undoubtedly true, regardless of the ideas of the world which are so intimate to our minds), we might suppose an Evil Genius who is deceiving me in every possible way. Such a consideration serves as a reinvigoration to not be inclined to error by ideas of things that are doubtful. Descartes’ persistence in supposing such a deceiving metaphysical creature becomes his criterion to form an unshakable belief upon anything—even when it comes to his own existence!

The mythical creature ought to pass any perception or idea—hereafter, both perceptions and ideas will be referred to as ideas—to his mind, and Descartes cannot possibly be deceived by ideas of anything outside us brought to him, as sophisticatedly fabricated as it can get, for they are so far doubtful. But could the Evil Genius come to convince him that he is nothing? No, for if he is to be deceived, he must be at least something. Hence, the Evil Genius would never bring him to be nothing, and if he is something, he must exist. Therefore, he exists.

In spite of how appealing the argument seems to be, fair diverged objections arise about how Descartes is assuming too much regarding the nature of the meditator—i.e., the “I” in “I think, therefore I am”—by asking what inferences could be made about an idea when it occurs. The only thing that can possibly be said is “an idea has been experienced,” without the necessity of the thought-thinker association Descartes forced into existence. For the association of an idea with a thinker is a mere assumption made by Descartes and an ill-inferred statement from non-existent knowledge about the nature of things. To many later philosophers, saying “an idea has been experienced requires a thinker” is tantamount to saying “rain has been experienced requires a rainer,” which is utterly absurd. It appears to be a linguistic label-assignment of two objects—the idea and the thinker—without a proper argument for the existence of such a relation. Any Cartesian defense in this regard stating the idea-thought connection is derived from the awareness of himself thinking fails; at most, introspection reveals nothing but the awareness of thought, which is itself another idea, and shows no connection to a subject of thought. This defense relocates the problem rather than solving it.

Further inquiry on this “I” leads to a possible question: what is the nature of this “I”? The statement “I think, therefore I exist” does not imply at all for this “I” to exist at any time before or after the idea has been experienced. In fact, the notion of time is ripped off from the criterion for existence, which seems necessary for any strong conviction of existence. When an idea is experienced, it stands self-contained, holding all its information at a single point in time, and the passage of time is only defined by successor ideas which might be assigned to other “I”s. Unlike Descartes’ internalist theory, adopting a notion of external time (independent of the succession of ideas) invokes an externalist view of the world which seems to offer a solution to the problems presented here.

The Cartesian legacy comes to an end with the invitation from Queen Christina of Sweden in 1649. After continuous hesitation, he accepted the invitation to tutor the Queen. Bacterial inflammation perished the fifty-four-year-old philosophy giant on February 11, 1650, under Stockholm’s winter. Many speculations about his death pivoted around the Scandinavian church—more specifically, symptoms of a Holy Poisoned Wafer unused to the Swedish chill after spreading what seemed to the church’s holy values to be ‘heretic opinions’. Needlessly to say, Descartes’ influence on subsequent philosophies and philosophers is unmatched; their works are indeed augmentations to his fundamental foundations. He is, indeed, ‘the father of modern philosophy.’
