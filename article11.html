The Lily of the Field and the Bird of the Air: A Book Review and Personal Reflection

By: Zainab Ammar

In this article we are going to discuss the concepts of the philosophical book The Lily of the Field and the Bird of the Air by Søren Kierkegaard, and how its main concepts remain relevant whether you believe in God or not. What I personally admired about this book is how it encourages the reader to behold the beauties of nature in silence, to sit still and listen to the world around us with complete devotion and grace, endeavoring to comprehend the similarities between us and nature. Kierkegaard used the remarkable metaphors of the lily and the bird to describe silence, obedience, and how in the process they bring joy. Let’s dive deeper into his ideas!

The Silence and Obedience of the Lily

Humans differ much from the lily: they love to talk about their strifes, and they like to complain and have meaningless, blank conversations lacking any kind of purpose other than ridiculous nonsense. The lily stands still in the field; it neither seeks attention nor compassion. It does not demand a change of placement or question why it was planted in that specific field, whether it was a grand garden or a hidden ditch. The lily does not compare its petals to the flower next to it, and most importantly, it does not question why it must endure its ritual. It waits in absolute silence and obeys the rules of nature with no bitterness.

The Song and Obedience of the Bird

The bird is one of the bravest creatures on earth. When seasons change, the bird flies away; it does not debate the path, neither delaying the journey nor pondering if it has enough courage to fly away—it simply flies away. The bird finds joy in existing; it sings its song even when it is facing a cruel winter, never stopping. The bird does not complain in its song, nor does it sing of selfish and mortal desires. The bird's song is a form of obedience and surrender to nature.

In Conclusion

What Kierkegaard wanted us to capture and learn from his book is the act of becoming one with nature: looking up to the bird and the lily and reflecting on what we have learned from them. We learn to keep silent, but not to always keep silent (for sometimes it is past endurance), and to not only speak of selfish, mortal desires. We learn to not always complain about our paths, but to accept them and step bravely into them, enjoying our journey as we remain on earth.

We must learn to love and appreciate who we are and what we ought to be, to obey and listen with every beat of our hearts, and to trust in nature. For we are nothing but mortals, and sometimes our unfathomable ego creeps into our minds and hearts to make us think that we have plenty of time on this earth. In reality, all we have are very brief moments, and for that, we ought to make them worth living and remembering by being unshaken and unafraid of decay—by simply accepting our mortality.
