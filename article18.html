Inside The Bell Jar

By: Malak Sinan

Through a thick glass of a bell jar, Sylvia Plath imparted the slow undergoing of descending into what is so called depression through her solitaire novel The Bell Jar, which she published under a fake name a month before her death. Now tell me dear reader, have you ever felt inadequate?

“The trouble was, I had been inadequate all along, I simply hadn't thought about it.”

— Sylvia Plath, The Bell Jar

Observant rather than engaged, and every bite you take tastes as nothing as tasting a paper, then right in front of your eyes, your life takes the shape of a fig tree where every branch is a path of a certain life choice, career, marriage... etc. While deciding which fig you were going to eat, they all rot and tumble before you as you starve.

“I saw my life branching out before me like the green fig tree in the story. From the tip of every branch, like a fat purple fig, a wonderful future beckoned and winked... just because I couldn't make up my mind which of the figs I would choose. I wanted each and every one of them, but choosing one meant losing all the rest, and, as I sat there, unable to decide, the figs began to wrinkle and go black, and, one by one, they plopped to the ground at my feet.”

— Sylvia Plath, The Bell Jar

In my opinion, most humans—especially women—relate to this fig tree analogy. As women, we relate to it due to society forcing us into molds that we never chose—the shape of whatever role they’d like us to be fixed in—and as humans with the curse of a limited single life. That was the beginning of Esther Greenwood's—the narrator's—crawl inside the bell jar.

Now why is it called the bell jar?

A bell jar is a glass container with a bell-shaped top created to cover objects in an enclosed environment. Now imagine you're inside this glass container watching the world around you rotate while you’re breathing your own air. That’s how Plath described the mental status of the narrator and in fact the mental status of Sylvia herself once you get to look through her life, her poetry, her and Esther's failed shocking treatments, and their identical failed suicide attempt; you will realize that it's safe to say that The Bell Jar is also an autobiographical novel and a reflection of Sylvia herself.

Where they unfortunately don't meet is the way the book ends. Esther felt her heart beating again, she was finally breathing feeling alive and outside the bell jar:

“I took a deep breath and listened to the old brag of my heart. I am, I am, I am.”

— Sylvia Plath, The Bell Jar

But realistically enough, she did not portray Esther’s release from the bell jar as a happy ultimate end; she was left filled with uncertainty that this feeling—the bell jar—will come back again, ending the book with that realistic end. Sylvia must've had her answer a short while after writing the last line, given she stuck her head in an oven in 1963 a month later after the book release, leaving her legacy behind alongside the breakfast she left for her kids that morning.

At last I must clarify: was Plath a prodigy or a racist? Now although my beloved Sylvia Plath made some feminist comments and expressed some feminist ideas, I believe her feminism is restricted to white women mostly, and that is called white feminism and it's not feminism at all in my opinion. And yes, I must admit she was a racist deliberately and made racist comments every now and then, and that is the flaw—that I do not deny—within her marvellous writing and perfect display—that I cannot deny—of mental health and depression through all her work. So I would call that “a flawed prodigy.”
