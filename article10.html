Wormholes: Science Fiction or Reality

By: Zainab Ammar

We have all seen wormholes in science fiction movies and how they allow the characters to travel vast cosmic distances instantly but ever wonder what is the scientific basis behind them? In this article we will discuss wormholes and their scientific origin.

So let's start with defining them! Wormholes are tunnel-like hypothetical structures that connect separate points in spacetime exactly like a magical portal that creates shortcuts between distant regions of the universe.

Now let's go back to the very roots of this concept; it was originally produced by the theory of general relativity in 1915, which stated that mass can curve spacetime, which will affect the movement of matter and light in the process. Let me help you imagine this: we all know that according to general relativity, spacetime has a fabric.

So let's imagine that fabric with planets placed in it, such as balls; if that ball is heavy, the fabric sinks and forms a deep bowl shape. This is how it affects mass.

If we took a smaller ball and decided to roll it, it would roll and circle around the heavy ball. To an outside observer, they look like they're attracting each other by such an anonymous force, but they're not! The small ball is simply just following the curved shape of that fabric.

Now you're wondering, how does it affect light? Well, we all know that light travels in a straight line, but what if the ground light is traveling on is curved? Then the light has to bend with the curve.

I know you're probably going to ask, "What does that have to do with wormholes?" Excellent question; let me answer it.

A wormhole is basically the result of that bending, or should I say the extreme version of it? Two places in the universe can be millions of light-years apart, but somehow the wormhole connects them. How is that? Well, Einstein proved that spacetime is flexible, so physicists realized you could theoretically bend the fabric of space until those two distant points are touching, but how? Grab a piece of paper, cut two holes in it, then bend that paper and insert a pencil in it! That pencil is the wormhole. The pencil hasn't traveled faster than light; it simply just used a shortcut by wrapping spacetime.

The Mathematical Journey of Wormholes

After Einstein came up with general relativity in 1915, a physicist named Karl Schwarzschild used Einstein's equations (the field equations) to prove that if you put enough mass in a tiny spot, space will bend to the level that nothing, not even light, can escape, which was the mathematical discovery of black holes.

In 1916 another physicist named Ludwig Flamm checked Schwarzschild's math and came up with another remarkable idea. He noticed that those equations allow an exact opposite thing, which is called a white hole; it's like the good and the evil twin: a black hole is something that absorbs and swallows everything (the evil twin), while a white hole is something that releases things (the good twin).

After that in the 1930s to be precise, Albert Einstein and his colleague Nathan Rosen took a look on Flamm's paper. They realized that it was mathematically possible to bridge those two things together—a black hole and a white hole—to basically connect them with a tunnel, which was later called the Einstein-Rosen bridge. The idea is the following: matter in a specific place in the universe falls into a black hole, gets absorbed, travels through the tunnel, and exits from a white hole in a completely different region in space.

The Instability and Unavailability of That Bridge

Researchers later on agreed an Einstein-Rosen bridge is unstable and can collapse immediately after forming, which would destroy anything attempting to pass through it, so what can we do to stabilize it? Stabilizing a wormhole requires hypothetical exotic matter with negative energy that repels gravity, and guess what? Such matter does not exist naturally! (It can only be made in labs—tiny amounts of it, though.) And even if it happened and we were able to stabilize it somehow, the presence of normal matter, such as a human being or a spaceship, could still trigger an immediate collapse.

The Holographic Wormhole Experiment Using a Quantum Computer

In 2022 physicists created the first wormhole ever; the team was led by Maria Spiropulu of the California Institute of Technology. So what was the experiment? The researchers used a concept named the "holographic principle," which states that a gravity system like a wormhole can be mathematically equivalent to a quantum particle system.

Let's try to explain this simply: the holographic principle states that 3D space, gravity, and everything we experience can be perfectly described by a 2D boundary of quantum particles surrounding that space. Now you're probably thinking, what does that even mean? Let's imagine a 3D game that you coded. That 3D game doesn't exist in reality; it's only on your computer, and it can be described by the code! The code is the quantum particles that surround the universe, and the world we live in is the 3D game.

We can also say that the hologram describes a 3D world containing gravity, black holes, and bendy spacetime (Einstein's General Relativity), while the surface describes a flat 2D world of quantum particles bouncing around without any gravity at all (Quantum Mechanics). Now let's go back to the experiment! That experiment relied heavily on the holographic principle and the ER=EPR conjecture, which posits that quantum entanglement and physical wormholes are two different mathematical descriptions of the exact same underlying reality. Therefore, sending information across highly entangled systems is equivalent to passing information through a wormhole. Fascinating, isn't it?

The researchers set up two isolated quantum systems on the chip: a left system and a right system. Each system consisted of 7 qubits (representing particles inside a black hole). These two sides were put into a highly specific entangled state called a Thermofield Double state. In the gravity description, this entanglement creates the two "mouths" of a closed wormhole. The left system was mathematically evolved backward in time using its quantum logic gates.

An 8th qubit—the probe qubit containing the message—was swapped into the left system. A 9th reference qubit remained outside to monitor the information. Later, the left system was evolved forward in time. This forward evolution caused the information of the probe qubit to instantly spread out chaotically across all the other qubits on the left side. In the gravity picture, this is equivalent to a particle falling into the left black hole and being torn apart. As mentioned earlier, wormholes are unstable and collapse before anything can cross them. To allow traversal, a negative energy shockwave is needed to open the wormhole.

To simulate this, the scientists applied a brief coupling pulse (an electric/magnetic field shift) across the left and right systems. They ran the test two ways: applying a pulse that simulated positive energy, and one that simulated negative energy. After the negative energy pulse was applied, the right system was allowed to evolve forward in time. Because of a unique mechanism called perfect size winding, the chaotic scrambling process effectively ran in reverse on the right side, much like a spilled drop of ink gathering back together. The scrambled information focused back into a single qubit on the right side. The researchers then measured the entanglement between this final qubit and the original reference qubit to see if the message survived.

The Result of the Experiment

The experiment was successful because the probe qubit successfully emerged on the right side! This occurred only when the negative pulse was applied, which matches the mathematical predictions of traversable wormhole dynamics perfectly!

However, despite how remarkable that experiment was, in 2023 independent physicists argued that while the physics of the experiment were a fascinating feat of quantum teleportation, the 9 qubit model used was too simple to be considered a true gravitational wormhole.

Despite the mathematical proofs of their existence, wormholes remain a debatable mystery in physics. Some consider them fictional, while others differ. I hope you enjoyed reading this short article as much as I enjoyed writing it!
