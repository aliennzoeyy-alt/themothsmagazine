How Quantum Mechanics Suggest The Multiverse As a Possible Interpretation of Reality?

By: Ahmed Mustafa

Quantum mechanics serves as the most accomplished theory of all time. Indeed, it has been rendered useful and sufficient to describe a variety of phenomena that used to be mysterious and perplexing without the quantum eye. The warm reddish sunset from the same azure sky, our burning sun itself! All of these phenomena are within the explanatory power of quantum mechanics. The curious reader would be enchanted by how the universe is comprehensively described by a manmade theory. And, in a twinkling of an eye, he stumbled upon the seemingly paradoxical tenets of the theory, where basic and primary human perceptions fail to fit, and on top of that, being incompatible with it. For the unfamiliar reader, quantum mechanics was grounded upon a collection of strikingly non-intuitive results that cannot be described in classical physics terms.

Where is the conflict between old and new physics?

In classical physics, an object acquires a number of properties, such as where it is located at a given time (confined in some space), how much the object is moving (velocity), and the environment in which the object interacts with. This set of properties defines what we call the classical state of a physical object, and this state develops and changes by the laws of classical physics in a completely deterministic fashion; that is, any later state of the same physical object is uniquely determined by its earlier states. These axioms, presented by Newton’s work and its successor modification, later were the central philosophical debates of whether they construct a true knowledge about reality. Immanuel Kant, in his book Critique of Pure Reason, argued that any possible knowledge about reality must be understood in the purely classical axiomatic formulation. Quantum mechanics reinvigorate a new formulation in which the assignment of any object to a set of properties cannot take place. For instance, if you measure the position of an electron to a great degree of precision, it implies you cannot know how much it is moving with small error. Now the careful reader might suggest that doesn’t exploit the classical picture. Because simply the position measurement’s device might interact uncontrollably with its motion, leading to a non-possible precise measurement of its motion. That is, the electron really has a precise position and velocity, with us being shorthanded to simultaneously measure them both precisely. This objection falls flat to reconcile the outcomes of the double slits experiment when it is performed on electrons. Imagine two sources of water waves, which are forks that vibrate with some frequency, turned on. Then the stirred water starts to show apparent wavy rises from the water surface; the two waves at some point will meet, showing a distinct interference pattern. Now, to perform a similar experiment on electrons, we’ll need two slits to make them pass through. Two findings are to be noticed. Firstly, when you eject an electron towards the slits without knowing which slit it passed, the electron seems to choose random points on the detection screen. After repeating the experiment on more electrons, an interference pattern shows on the screen similar to that one of waves! Which seems to be contrary to the nature of electrons, being particles with masses. Secondly, when you place a detecting device in front of one of the slits, you ought to tell when the electron passes the slit or not. Repeating the experiment again and again, and no interference pattern shows. These two parts of the experiment guarantee that if you assign the classical properties to the electron, the first part cannot happen. And if you assign the properties of waves to the electron, the second part cannot happen. This, of course, doesn’t mean the electron eavesdropping you and your lab colleagues do, whether you put a detector on the slit or not. But it realizes the necessity of a new theory that is not classical in nature.

Could the disappointment with the new physics be resolved with the multiverse?

The new quantum state—hereafter it will be referred to as the wave function—gives you all possible states you’ll be observing on the screen, assigning each possible outcome a probability. This means the wave function does not guarantee which outcome you’ll observe for a single electron but does guarantee the statistical mean of repeated observations. Another necessary monolith of the quantum theory is the collapse of the wave function: The wave function of a particle that is not subject to an observer develops in a smooth, mellow fashion; once it is observed, everything collapses into one single classical state. The questions about this conflict between the two natures of the wave function are, what is the physical mechanism holding the choice of the possible outcomes? Or if there are any mechanisms at all? And why? Are all labeled under the Measurement Problem, which, as we shall see later, is resolved in the Multiverse Interpretation?

Albert Einstein, who is one of the founders of the quantum theory, detested the abducted fine structure from the wave function. For the wave function is to be understood in observer-outcome contexts without any underlining, more fundamental meaning. Whereas the scientific enterprise's ultimate goal was to unravel the yet-unknown aspects of nature without needing any notion of any acquaintance of an observer. Einstein's disappointment with the new theory grew more to reach the roots of the theory until it reached its final form in 1935 by the release of the EPR paper, short for Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen, titled “Can Quantum Mechanical Description of Physical Reality Be Considered Complete?” The paper concludes that quantum mechanics cannot maintain both locality and completeness consistently: Locality is defined as the fact that information cannot be transferred instantaneously between two physical objects, whilst completeness of a theory is defined as the set of the elements of reality of an object being all contained within the theory. Einstein developed an idiosyncrasy inherited from his work on relativity that locality is tied up with how the universe works, making him willing to give up completeness for locality. Einstein concludes that quantum mechanics is incomplete. Unfortunately, later on, in 1964, John S. Bell, a native of Northern Ireland and a staff member of CERN, published a paper showing that any theory maintaining both incompleteness and locality is doomed to not produce any results compatible with laboratory findings. That, however, doesn’t resolve the doubt of whether the wave function is of hidden reality with a nonlocal nature or whether the wave function, as it is, represents the reality of our universe. A possible interpretation of the theory is the Multiverse Interpretation, which takes consistent sophistication in the trade of philosophical economy. The interpretation is grounded on two positive answers to two questions: does the wave function represent some reality of the system and not a state of imperfect knowledge? And does the theory have no hidden variables, in other words, complete? If you say yes to these two questions, you are then left to describe what is the nature of this wave function. The idea of this interpretation is that there is a multitude of worlds within the universe; each observer gets to see only one classical world when a quantum experiment is performed, with all the other possible outcomes lying in newly created parallel worlds.

To outline the latter, every single outcome of the experiment would correspond to a newer reality in which the possible outcome takes place. This, indeed, resolves the measurement problem, for none of the possible outcomes suddenly disappear, and there is no mechanism of choice for which of them are realized, but all of them are equally real. This manifests how a scientific enterprise might follow a non-linear route and not motivate comprehensive building blocks, rather sometimes taking a detour into a very abhorrent and pragmatic formulation without one interpretation of the theory, leading to the weirdest and most beautiful ideas under the pressure of such heavy mental tasks, making our quest for knowledge as human as it can be.
