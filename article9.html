The Scarlet Letter: A Book Review

By: Zainab Ammar

Written by Nathaniel Hawthorne and published in 1850, the scarlet letter is a classic historical novel set in a strict 17th-century Puritan community in Boston, Massachusetts. That book was the most gut-wrenching read of the year to me. It tells a melancholic tale of a woman named Hester Prynne, who sinned in an age where women were treated like subhumans. The town forces her to wear a bright red letter "A" on her chest as a symbol of shame for committing adultery. Hester carries the weight of that sin alone, even though she did not commit it alone. She chooses to carry that burden all by herself, fearing to expose her partner in crime and ruin his life.

The novel opens in Boston with Hester Prynne standing on the town scaffold. Carrying her infant daughter, Pearl, she faces the judgment of her community a community full of hypocrites who play God. Because her husband was long lost at sea and presumed dead, her pregnancy exposes her affair. On the very day of her trial, her husband arrives, adopting a fake identity under the name of Roger Chillingworth. After witnessing this, Roger makes it his life's goal to find Hester's lover, forcing Hester to keep his true identity a secret.

This book dives into the hypocritical ways of thinking in the Puritan community how they judge and shame people who commit sins in public, while their masters commit the exact same sins in secret. But what is even the definition of a sin? And why must you endure the consequences of a small action for the rest of your life? Is it even fair?

What was mind boggling later in the book is the fact that Hester's secret lover and the father of Pearl is Arthur Dimmesdale, the town's highly revered, pious young minister. While Hester endures public exile and humiliation, Dimmesdale suffers in silence, devoured by overwhelming guilt. This mental state leads him to suffer from a physical illness despite his endeavors to conceal his guilt. Sensing his hidden torment, Chillingworth presents himself as Dimmesdale's doctor just to torture him psychologically. In the process, he discovers a strange, self inflicted symbol on Dimmesdale's chest. He realizes that his apprehensions are true and that Dimmesdale is, indeed, Hester's secret lover.

On the other hand, Hester faces a lot of difficulties, starting with the odd behavior of her wild child, Pearl, and how abnormally aggressive she was. A truly magnificent daughter, she does not obey the rules but questions them a curious, phenomenal child who never cared about how people in town perceived her mother. She loved her mother, cherished her, and loved her scarlet letter. What I personally loved about the whole letter thing is how it represented different things throughout the novel. To me, that letter was the very heart of this story.

However, after seven long years of suffering, Hester and Dimmesdale meet secretly in the forest and plan to escape to Europe together. But before they can leave, Dimmesdale decides to go to the scaffold after delivering his final sermon. He publicly confesses his sin, reveals the self inflicted mark on his chest, and dies in Hester arms. The town is horribly shocked by this event because of the fact that someone they looked up to, and considered a saint in a way, was also a sinner.

Deprived of his revenge, Chillingworth dies shortly after. Hester and Pearl leave the colony, but Hester eventually returns to the Puritan colony years later. She voluntarily puts the scarlet letter back on and dedicates herself to supporting women who suffer from societal judgment and emotional struggles. Rather than she turns her own cottage into a sanctuary for women burdened by hidden sorrows. Because she has lived through the ultimate public shaming and survived, she listens without the harsh judgment of the Puritan elders. I've got to admit that this was my favorite part of the novel how Hester was capable of turning a symbol of shame into a symbol of sympathy and honor.

And that was the end! Thanks for reading.
