Misogyny in STEM

By: Sama Falah

It is 9 am on a Thursday morning, and I stand with my back hunched over some tiresome thermodynamics experiment; I hear my professor showering someone with compliments. Seeing that I have been left to solo the work, I lift my eyes fully expecting to be the object of the praise only to find my classmates are giggling and chatting as the old professor is going on and on about how smart, hardworking and oh so helpful my male classmate is, and I find myself wondering if my biggest mistake in life is not being born a man.

Through the past two years of me being in college I found myself a target of misogyny and discrimination so many times that it slowly became a daily routine. Women in STEM all around the world fight everyday to be seen and credited—sadly the fight here in our country is ten times harder. When people see me, a woman studying physics, they see a teacher; when they see a man studying physics they see a scientist or a professor. It sometimes feels like they see women as a sub-species rather than functioning human beings.

The problem with misogyny in workplaces or educational institutes is the fact that it is not always this grand woman-hating act. Most often than not it shows up through very minuscule behaviors and words, that it almost makes you sound crazy to point out. For instance, in my first year of college the professor was trying to explain a mechanics principle about cars, so he turned to the boys in class and said that they are the only ones that will understand this topic because “the girls know nothing about cars.” I remember telling my female classmates about how weird his remark was and them saying I was just being a dramatic feminist and how our professor is a good man who means no harm. Which reminds us that misogyny is oftentimes kept alive through women—they sadly uphold the patriarchy and reinforce it even if it directly harms them, because they firmly believe this harm is what is natural and what they are meant for.

Misogyny in STEM continues to hinder women and discourage their innovation. Whether through harmful stereotypes, discrimination, or unequal opportunities, its impact leaves women feeling invisible and further alienated from the science scene. The STEM community has so much work to do to progress into a more inclusive space that utilizes working hands from both genders.
