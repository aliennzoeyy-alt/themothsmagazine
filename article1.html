The Art of Spectroscopy

By: Zainab Ammar

Ever gazed at the sky and wondered how scientists knew what stars are made of? It's not like you can climb up the sky and get one and put it in a laboratory! So how did they exactly do it? In this article, I will make it my duty to answer all your questions and tell you that story..

Once upon a time in the 17th century, to be specific, there was a guy named Isaac Newton. Isaac took a prism and put it in front of the window, allowing the beams of sunlight to pass through it, but that wasn't the case. The beams of light did not just pass through the prism; the beam split into a rainbow of colors! So the white light we see every day wasn't white at all; that was only a disguise. It is a combination of all those colors. Quite fascinating, isn't it? This is called spectroscopy, which is simply defined as the science of analyzing light by breaking it into its spectrum. What was odd about this discovery is that scientists later on discovered that when light passes through a prism, you do not only observe a continuous rainbow; there are also some mysterious dark lines, which will lead us to part two of this story.

Once upon a time in the 19th century, to be specific, a guy named Joseph von Fraunhofer was experimenting with lenses to improve the quality of optical glass, so Joseph used a telescope and prism, which helped him analyze sunlight, and he observed that there are hundreds of those mysterious dark lines that are crossing the rainbow of light, so Joseph thought to himself, "Something is removing specific colors of light? What could it possibly be?" So in the process, those mysterious dark lines were named after him: Fraunhofer lines.

The strife to figure out what was removing certain colors of light continued to puzzle scientists until the mid-1800s, when Gustav Kirchhoff and Robert Bunsen were experimenting with chemical elements and flames. What they discovered was that each and every chemical element produces a unique set of colored lines in its spectrum. Now, where does this lead us? Kirchhoff realized that these same patterns appeared in the sun's spectrum, but instead of bright lights, they appeared as dark lines. You're probably wondering now what's the reason behind this.

Well I got you. Inside our sun, the dense, incredibly hot core and lower layer of atmosphere (which is called the photosphere) emit light of almost all wavelengths. If this light came straight forward to our eyes, we would see a continuous spectrum containing all the bright colors, but that simply is not possible, for before the light can escape into outer space, it has to pass through the sun's outer, cooler layer of atmosphere (which is called the chromosphere). This layer is full of various gaseous elements like hydrogen, helium, sodium, and iron. The atoms in that cooler gas are called "picky eaters"—as we all know, electrons can only exist in specific energy levels. An electron can absorb a photon of light to jump to a higher energy level, so when light passes through the cooler gas, the atoms absorb those very specific wavelengths of light that match their electron transitions (the dark lines), and the rest of the light passes through as it is. Now you're probably asking yourself: when the atom absorbs light, it will eventually re-emit it when the electrons drop back down, so why does the light disappear?

Well yes, the atoms do re-emit that light, but they scatter it in all directions across space and not in a straight line traveling directly towards a telescope. Now you're about to ask another question: since there are multiple dark lines, how can we tell which is which? Listen carefully; every element leaves a unique fingerprint of dark lines, so by matching the dark lines in the sun's spectrum to known absorption lines of the chemical elements in a laboratory, we can determine what stars are made of! That's it; that's the whole thing. Thanks for reading!
