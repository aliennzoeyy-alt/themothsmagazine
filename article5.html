The Ongoing Struggle of Women in Afghanistan

By: Zainab Ammar

In this article, we are going to talk about the women's rights crisis under the ruling Taliban in Afghanistan, so sit tight and read.

To inform you of what's happening, I have to start from the very roots of this topic. So who are the Taliban? Known as the Islamic Movement of Taliban, they're basically the ruling government of Afghanistan now—an ultraconservative political and religious organization that took over 90% of Afghanistan from 1996 to 2001. Then as always, guess who intervened? The United States, of course! They took them down after the September 11 attacks (of course it is about them; you don't seriously think they actually care about how the Taliban are a terror organization?). However, that strife went on for 20 years, until the coalition forces (which are the American forces who led the mission in Afghanistan) decided to withdraw and depart in August 2021. They signed a deal called the United States–Taliban deal, and guess what happened? They left, and the Taliban took over ALL of Afghanistan.

Now, let's talk about what women in Afghanistan have been experiencing ever since. Imagine being a fully functioning human being, but everyone around you is dehumanizing you to the level that they treat you like a pet—except that they don't even take care of you. Quite the contrary: they humiliate you and strip you of your basic needs to continue living, and all you ever do every day, every minute, is endure.

That is exactly what it is like to be a woman in Afghanistan.

Women's rights have been severely restricted. They stripped them of basic human rights and dignity, and denied them the right to education. They are not allowed to study or to work. They are not allowed to even leave their houses! They even restricted their movement and public decision-making—they're not allowed to go to parks or gyms. They can't even access healthcare facilities cleanly because they simply don't allow women to work, and at the same time, they don't allow women to be examined by a male doctor. In December 2024, they banned women from studying medicine and midwifery. Women there are living a far less healthy life, not to mention the rising adolescent birth rates due to child marriage.

This isn't just about women's rights; it's about basic human rights and decency.

Statistics from the UN show that:

  • 78% of young Afghan women are not in education, employment, or training.
  • Early childbearing is projected to rise by 45%.
  • Maternal mortality could increase by more than 50%.
