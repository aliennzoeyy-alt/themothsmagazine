Moths: Seekers of Light

By: Nyr Elhussain

Whether you see them hovering around lamplights at night, or at the corners of the streets, or on the title and cover of our magazine, moths are a familiar spectacle for those of us who are observant enough to take notice of them; their fluffy wings, monochromatic colors, and grim air distinguish them immensely from their fellow insects.

Moths are generally classified as any non-butterfly species within the Lepidopteran order of insects, which means they're closely related to butterflies but are quite different from them in some aspects. There are an estimated 160,000 species of moths in the wild and in our urban areas—compared to only 17,500 species of butterflies—a staggering number of unique insects scattered all around the world.

Butterflies and moths might be related but they possess certain traits that set them apart: butterflies are diurnal insects whose body, usually but not necessarily, is pigmented with vivid colors, whilst moths are mostly nocturnal and tend to be less colorful than their butterfly counterparts. But the main difference that scientists usually use to distinguish between the two is that moths tend to have straight feathery or thread-like antennae, in contrast to butterflies, which have slightly curled club-tipped antennae.

A moth's body, just like its butterfly relatives, is composed of three main segments: the head, the thorax, and the abdomen. The head contains a pair of compound eyes—similar to those in flies, mosquitoes, etc.—and a long, coiled proboscis (a long, thin tongue) that can uncoil to collect nectar from flowers or other fluids that they feed on.

The thorax is subdivided into three sections, each of which contains a pair of legs. The two back sections of the thorax—known as the mesothorax and metathorax—bear a pair of forewings and hindwings, respectively. Their antennae, palps, and legs are studded with sense receptors that are used to smell, and their wings are covered with very tiny scales that look like powder to the naked eye.

The abdomen, which is the furthest part of the moth's body on the back, consists of ten little segments that contain the digestive, respiratory, and reproductive organs.

Moths are holometabolous insects, which means they undergo full metamorphosis in four stages throughout their life—one of the most fascinating characteristics in a moth's life. They start out as a tiny egg that hatches into a larva (also known as a caterpillar), which will in turn become a pupa entrapped within a silk cocoon that will eventually be torn apart by the adult moth once it's ready to fly out into the air.

Most moths have short lives that last for a few days after hatching from their cocoon, during which they mate and lay their eggs—usually close to food sources that their caterpillars can consume. However, certain moths can live up to a few weeks in their adult form if predators—like bats, lizards, spiders, etc.—don't cut their lives short.

Many moths mainly feed on nectar, which they use their proboscis to collect from the base of flower petals, in the process pollinating the flowers and helping them thrive. Certain moth species obtain their nutritious needs from fresh or fermented fruits, tree sap, or even blood. Yes, you read right—about eight different species of moths suck the blood out of mammals as their means to survive (that's more than how many vampire bats we know of!).
